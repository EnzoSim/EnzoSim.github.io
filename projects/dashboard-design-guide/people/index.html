The people

None of the rules in this guide was invented for dashboards. They come from people who spent careers on one part of the problem: how to divide a page, shade a map, keep an operator oriented, report a business, measure what a reader sees, and make a control answer.

Grid and type · 1964

Karl Gerstner

1930–2017 · Swiss designer

Write the rules first, and let the layouts follow from them.

Projects
  1. 1958Gerstner + Kutter The Basel advertising agency he founded with Markus Kutter, renamed GGK when Paul Gredinger joined in 1962.
  2. 1962Capital grid A layout grid for the business magazine Capital, 58 units wide with 2-unit gutters, that carries one to six columns of whole units.
  3. 1964Designing Programmes Essays on design as the writing of a programme of rules from which many solutions follow.
  4. 1972Compendium for Literates A systematic analysis of letterforms and the variables a designer can change, published in German as Kompendium für Alphabeten.
What they brought

He argued that a designer should first write the rules of a problem as a programme, and let those rules produce the individual solutions. His grid for Capital made the idea concrete: one module chosen so that every column count from one to six falls on whole units.

What to learn from them

Before choosing column widths, pick a field whose width plus one gutter divides by every column count you need: Capital’s 58 units plus a 2-unit gutter make 60, which divides by one to six. Then give the important regions wider spans of that one grid.

Reporting and statistics · 2006

Stephen Few

American consultant and author

Small linear instruments, read at a glance.

Projects
  1. 2004Show Me the Numbers A book on designing tables and graphs for business communication.
  2. 2006Information Dashboard Design A book on the visual design of business dashboards.
  3. 2006Bullet Graph Design Specification The definition of the bullet graph: a label, a linear scale, a featured measure, up to two comparative measures and two to five qualitative ranges, revised in 2013.
  4. 2008With Dashboards, Formatting and Layout Definitely Matter A paper on dashboard layout that counts the rigid symmetrical grid among common mistakes.
What they brought

He translated perception research and Tufte’s principles into practical rules for the tables, graphs and dashboards of business reporting. He designed compact forms for monitoring screens, above all the bullet graph, to replace gauges and meters that take much space and show little.

What to learn from them

Replace each gauge with a bar on a linear scale and a tick for the comparator, and size dashboard regions by importance, so the main evidence gets a wide region and secondary measures narrow ones.

Reporting and statistics · 1983

Edward Tufte

born 1942 · American statistician

Spend ink on data, and erase what carries none.

Projects
  1. 1983The Visual Display of Quantitative Information A self-published book on the principles and history of statistical graphics, which introduced data-ink and chartjunk.
  2. 1990Envisioning Information A book on showing many dimensions on flat paper, with chapters on layering and separation and on small multiples.
  3. 1997Visual Explanations A book on showing motion, process, cause and effect.
  4. 2006Beautiful Evidence A book on presenting evidence, which named the sparkline.
What they brought

He set out principles for statistical graphics with examples from two centuries of practice: show the data, remove ink that carries no information, compare small multiples on shared scales. The terms he coined or popularised, data-ink, chartjunk, small multiples and sparkline, gave practitioners a shared vocabulary for arguing about design.

What to learn from them

Set a word-sized sparkline of the last 12 to 26 periods beside each headline figure, with a grey band for the normal range where one exists, and delete any border, fill or grid line that carries no data.

Reporting and statistics · 1924

Walter Shewhart and Donald Wheeler

American statisticians

Separate routine variation from signals before reacting.

Projects
  1. 1924The control chart memorandum Shewhart’s one-page memo at Western Electric, with the first schematic control chart.
  2. 1931Economic Control of Quality of Manufactured Product Shewhart’s book on chance and assignable causes of variation and the state of statistical control.
  3. 1993Understanding Variation Wheeler’s short book applying process behaviour charts to the figures managers read every month.
What they brought

Shewhart gave industry a test for telling routine variation from a change worth investigating: limits computed from the process’s own data. Wheeler showed managers how to apply it to business figures with the chart of individual values and moving ranges.

What to learn from them

Before calling a week good or bad, compute natural process limits from a baseline of past values (the average plus and minus 2.66 times the average moving range) and flag only points outside them or long runs on one side of the average.

Reporting and statistics · 1786

William Playfair

1759–1823 · Scottish engineer and political economist

Draw the difference as an area, and nobody has to subtract.

Projects
  1. 1786The Commercial and Political Atlas Forty-three time-series charts of trade and finance, and one bar chart of Scotland’s trade with seventeen countries.
  2. 1801The Statistical Breviary A compilation of national statistics, with what are likely the first pie charts and circle graphs.
  3. 1805Statistical Account of the United States of America His translation of a book by D. F. Donnant, to which he added a pie chart of land by state.
What they brought

He showed economic data as graphs when such figures appeared only in tables, and argued that charts communicate better than tables. He drew the quantity that mattered as a shape, so the balance of trade became the tinted area between two lines.

What to learn from them

When a key figure is the difference between two series, such as plan and actual, plot both on one scale and fill the space between them with the gap labelled, so its size and sign over time can be read directly.

Cartography · 1967

Jacques Bertin

1918–2010 · French cartographer

Choose each visual variable for what it can express.

Projects
  1. 1954Laboratoire de cartographie The cartography laboratory of the École pratique des hautes études in Paris, which he founded and directed.
  2. 1967Sémiologie graphique A theory of graphics for diagrams, networks and maps, translated into English in 1983 as Semiology of Graphics.
  3. 1977La Graphique et le traitement graphique de l’information A book on graphics as a tool for analysing data, translated into English in 1981.
What they brought

He named the visual variables of a mark (position on the plane, size, value, texture, colour, orientation and shape) and stated which kinds of data each can carry. He treated a graphic as a tool for finding structure, permuting the rows and columns of a matrix until groups appear.

What to learn from them

Match each encoding to the data before drawing: lightness or size for ordered quantities, hue or shape for categories, position for anything that must be compared precisely. Sort rows and columns by a meaningful value so that patterns form blocks.

Perception and uncertainty · 1984

William Cleveland

1943–2026 · American statistician

Position along a common scale is read most accurately.

Projects
  1. 1979Loess A method of robust locally weighted regression for smoothing scatterplots.
  2. 1984Graphical perception With Robert McGill, experiments that ranked how accurately readers decode position, length, angle, area and shading.
  3. 1985The Elements of Graphing Data A book of principles for scientific graphs.
  4. 1996Trellis display With Richard Becker and Ming-Jen Shyu, panels conditioned on variables and set out with shared scales.
What they brought

He measured how accurately people read quantities from different encodings and ranked them, which gave design choices an experimental basis. At Bell Labs and later at Purdue he also developed loess smoothing and trellis displays, and helped to create the S language.

What to learn from them

Encode any quantity that must be compared precisely as position along a common scale, as aligned bars or dots, and keep angle, area and colour intensity for secondary or categorical information.

Reporting and statistics · 2015

Rolf Hichert

German consultant

Reports need a notation as fixed as a musical score.

Projects
  1. 2004HICHERT+PARTNER The consultancy he founded, today the IBCS Institute, which trains and certifies report designers.
  2. 2013IBCS Association The new association to which he transferred most of his work, and which publishes the standards for free use.
  3. 2015IBCS version 1.0 The first release of the International Business Communication Standards, approved by the association’s members in Amsterdam.
  4. 2019Solid, Outlined, Hatched A book with Jürgen Faisst on why reports need a notation and how to apply one to charts and tables.
What they brought

He gathered the advice of Playfair, Brinton, Tufte and Few into a standard that companies can adopt, with fixed meanings for fills, scenarios, scales and labels. He argued that business reports need a shared notation in the way music and engineering drawing have one.

What to learn from them

Fix one fill per scenario across every report (solid for actual, outlined for plan, hatched or lighter for forecast, grey for the previous period), so readers learn it once and hue stays free for highlighting.

Reporting and statistics · 1977

Andrew Ehrenberg

1926–2010 · British statistician

Round hard, order by size, compare down the column.

Projects
  1. 1972Repeat-Buying His account of the regular patterns in how consumers buy a brand again and again.
  2. 1975Data Reduction A book arguing that patterns in data are found, and shown, through simple tables.
  3. 1977Rudiments of numeracy A paper in the Journal of the Royal Statistical Society with his rules for tables of figures.
  4. 1984The Dirichlet model With Gerald Goodhardt and Chris Chatfield, a model of brand choice that accounts for regularities such as double jeopardy.
What they brought

He tested what makes a table of numbers easy to read and reduced it to a few rules: round hard, order by size, compare down columns, and give averages as a focus. He brought the same habits to marketing data and found regularities in buying behaviour that held across many markets.

What to learn from them

Round each figure to the two digits that vary, order rows by size, put the numbers to be compared in a column, and add a row or column average as a reference.

Interaction and forecasting · 1994

Ben Shneiderman

born 1947 · American computer scientist

Let people steer the data with visible, reversible controls.

Projects
  1. 1983Direct manipulation An article in IEEE Computer describing interfaces of visible objects and rapid, incremental, reversible actions.
  2. 1992Treemaps A space-filling layout of nested rectangles whose areas show size, first used for the files on a shared disk.
  3. 1994Dynamic queries Sliders and buttons tied to a display that updates as they move, so a query can be steered.
  4. 1996The Eyes Have It A taxonomy of visualization by data type and task, with the advice to start from an overview and give details on demand.
What they brought

He set out direct manipulation as the basis of usable interfaces: visible objects, rapid reversible actions and immediate feedback. With his lab he showed that controls tied to a live display let people explore data without writing queries, and he invented the treemap.

What to learn from them

Make every filter a visible control whose effect appears within about a tenth of a second and can be undone in one step; when an update takes longer, preview its effect before applying it.

Grid and type programmes

Designers who treated a layout or a typeface as a system of compatible parts.
13 people

  • Jan Tschichold

    1902–1974 · German typographer

    1928 Die neue Typographie

    He wrote the manifesto of modernist typography and later returned to classical book design, so his work covers both the asymmetric page and the quiet conventional one. His rules for Penguin showed that a short written standard can hold some 500 titles to one level of craft across many compositors.

    What to learn from themRemove the vertical rules from a table and widen the gaps between columns instead; keep a light horizontal rule only where rows are long enough for the eye to lose its line.

    Rule 8 · Set tables to be read

  • Adrian Frutiger

    1928–2015 · Swiss type designer

    1957 Univers

    He designed a typeface as one coordinated system, with every weight and width drawn and approved together and named by a code that gives its place in the family. In his signage and recognition faces he judged letters by how reliably they can be told apart in hard conditions: at a distance, at an angle, or by a machine.

    What to learn from themBuild a dashboard’s type from one family arranged as a small matrix of weight and width, and give each role one cell, for example a heavier weight for values and a narrower width for column heads. When a new kind of label appears, assign it an existing cell before adding a style.

    Rule 9 · Choose type for figures
  • ERNo free photograph

    Emil Ruder

    1914–1970 · Swiss typographer

    1942 Typography at the Allgemeine Gewerbeschule Basel

    He taught that the white space inside and around letters is as much a material as the letters themselves, and that rhythm and contrast come from controlling it. His manual carried the Basel approach of sans serif type, asymmetric layout and the grid into design schools in Europe and North America.

    What to learn from themTreat the spaces around figures as fixed tokens: one gap between a value and its unit, one between a sign and its digits, one between columns. Check them at the smallest size in use, since uneven gaps make a column look ragged even when its figures align.

    Rule 9 · Choose type for figures

  • Otl Aicher

    1922–1991 · German designer

    1953 Ulm School of Design

    He treated a visual identity as a system of rules for colour, type, grid and symbols that many people could apply to thousands of items and keep consistent. In Munich his department turned that system into information, from pictograms built on one grid to a schedule that cross-indexes dates, events and pictograms on a single sheet.

    What to learn from themWhen a screen lists events across categories, run one shared axis, such as dates, across every row and mark each event in its cell, so a reader finds any item by row and column. Draw all symbols on one construction grid so they read as a set.

    Rule 7 · Share one axis

  • Wim Crouwel

    1928–2019 · Dutch designer

    1963 Total Design

    He drew letters and pages from the constraints of production, from cathode-ray screens to computer typesetting, and let a strict grid order the content. His telephone directory showed that the order of fields can remove furniture: with the number next to the name, no dot leaders were needed.

    What to learn from themIn a dense list, place the two fields that are read together side by side, so no leader dots or rules are needed to join them, and test the result with readers. Total Design’s 1983 revision of the directory enlarged the type and cut the columns from four to three after readers complained that the type was too small.

    Rule 8 · Set tables to be read

  • Massimo Vignelli

    1931–2014 · Italian designer

    1970 New York City Transit Authority Graphics Standards Manual

    He showed how a grid, a title band and a few typefaces, fixed once, can carry varied content across hundreds of publications and keep them one family. His subway diagram put the clarity of lines and connections ahead of geography, and the public’s reaction to it showed where that choice reaches its limit.

    What to learn from themBefore designing any single view, write down the frame: the grid, the title band and two or three type sizes. Make each new view by filling that frame, and change the frame only when a view truly cannot fit it.

    Rule 1 · Start from a grid that divides

  • Matthew Carter

    born 1937 · British-born type designer

    1978 Bell Centennial

    He designed type around the way a medium fails, from ink spreading into newsprint to coarse pixels on early screens, so that the letters survive it. His faces for Microsoft were planned for the low resolution of 1990s displays and made small text on screen easier to read.

    What to learn from themTest candidate faces at the smallest size and on the worst display the dashboard will meet, such as a projector or a low-density laptop, and keep the face whose figures 3, 5, 6, 8 and 9 stay distinct there.

    Rule 9 · Choose type for figures

  • Josef Müller-Brockmann

    1914–1996 · Swiss designer

    1950 Tonhalle concert posters

    He turned the grid from studio practice into a method that could be taught, deriving fields and gutters from the type size and line spacing of the text. His concert posters showed that a strict structure can still carry movement and contrast.

    What to learn from themDerive the grid from the text: fix the body size and line height first, then make row heights and vertical gaps whole multiples of that line height, so charts, cards and text share one baseline rhythm.

    Rule 1 · Start from a grid that divides
  • RKNo free photograph

    Robin Kinross

    British typographer and writer

    1980 Hyphen Press

    He argued that every piece of information design takes a position, so a plain and orderly style is itself a rhetorical choice and should be made knowingly. As historian and publisher he tied modern typography to its arguments and contexts, including the Isotype work he studied for his graduate thesis.

    What to learn from themPut one line on every board that says what kind of numbers it shows (actual, plan, sample or model output), because a calm and exact layout lends authority to whatever figures it holds.

    Rule 12 · Use plain words, and say what the numbers are
  • JHNo free photograph

    Jost Hochuli

    born 1933 · Swiss book designer

    1979 VGS publishing association

    He treated the spacing of letters, words and lines as the place where reading succeeds or fails. He taught that care for three decades, in Zurich from 1967 and in St. Gallen from 1980 to 1996, and practised it in the books he designed.

    What to learn from themSet letter-spacing by role instead of accepting defaults: a little positive tracking for small capital labels, none for figures in a table, and check both at the size actually used.

    Rule 9 · Choose type for figures

  • Robert Bringhurst

    born 1946 · Canadian typographer and poet

    1991 The Form of the Book

    He gathered the craft of setting text into numbered rules, each with a reason and a history, covering spacing, proportion, figures, scale and the page. His book became a common reference for typographers, and its rules have been applied directly to typography on screen.

    What to learn from themPut a non-breaking space between every value and its unit so that no line break separates them, and choose a short scale of type sizes before designing any card, so every size on the screen comes from that list.

    Rule 9 · Choose type for figures

  • Tobias Frere-Jones

    born 1970 · American type designer

    1993 Interstate

    He drew typefaces for specific reading conditions, from highway signs to stock tables set at five and a half points, and treated the space between and inside letters as part of the drawing. Retina combined two devices for dense figures: one width across all weights, and ink traps that fill as the ink spreads.

    What to learn from themIf a selected or total row in a table will turn bold, choose a face whose bold figures keep the regular width, and check it by overlaying the two weights at the size you will use.

    Rule 9 · Choose type for figures

Cartography

Map makers who worked out how tone, strength and order carry hierarchy on a crowded sheet.
3 people

  • Arthur Robinson

    1915–2004 · American cartographer

    1941 OSS map division

    He argued that a map’s design should follow from its function, and that conventions such as north at the top need a reason. At Wisconsin from 1947 he built a programme that granted degrees in cartography and trained many who went on to found programmes elsewhere.

    What to learn from themBefore choosing tones, rank every element on the screen into three or four levels of importance, then give each level one weight or tone, so the main figure stands forward and frames, grid lines and labels recede.

    Rule 5 · Shade for hierarchy, print the quantity

  • Eduard Imhof

    1895–1986 · Swiss cartographer

    1925 Cartographic Institute, ETH Zurich

    He developed a way of shading relief that makes terrain read as form while leaving room for the other content of the map. His rules for colour, a muted ground for large areas and strong colour only in small ones, reached information design through Edward Tufte.

    What to learn from themKeep large surfaces such as the page, panels and table backgrounds in quiet low-saturation tones, and reserve full saturation for small marks that need attention, such as a value outside its range or the selected item.

    Rule 5 · Shade for hierarchy, print the quantity

Control-room engineering

Engineers who designed for operators who must notice a deviation at once.
3 people
  • DWNo free photograph

    David Woods

    American researcher, Ohio State University

    1983 Cognitive systems engineering

    From work on power-plant control rooms at Westinghouse he described how operators lose their way among many displays, and named the remedies: an overview, landmarks, overlap between views and spatial layout. With Erik Hollnagel he made the joint system of people and machines the unit of design.

    What to learn from themKeep recurring elements such as the date axis, the totals and the filters in fixed positions in every view, and repeat a compact overview on each detail screen, so a reader who moves between views does not have to search again.

    Rule 7 · Share one axis
  • JRNo free photographKVNo free photograph

    Jens Rasmussen and Kim Vicente

    Danish engineer, 1926–2018 · Canadian engineer

    1983 Skills, rules, knowledge

    They argued that an interface should show the physical and functional laws of the system it controls, so operators can reason from them when something unforeseen happens. Their framework matches kinds of display to levels of behaviour, so routine monitoring can run on perception and attention is left for solving problems.

    What to learn from themList the identities your figures must obey, such as opening stock plus receipts minus sales equals closing stock, and draw the most important one as geometry, so a broken constraint shows as a broken shape.

    Rule 4 · Draw the identity
  • BHNo free photograph

    Bill Hollifield and co-authors

    Process-control engineers

    2006 The Alarm Management Handbook

    They argued that bright, pictorial process screens hide the conditions operators most need to see, and proposed muted screens on which only abnormal states stand out. Their indicators show where a value sits against its normal range, so a deviation can be seen before a number is read.

    What to learn from themGive each key measure a short scale with its normal range drawn as a grey band, keep everything inside the range in neutral tones, and add colour only when a value leaves the band, so that colour on the screen always means look here.

    Rule 2 · Give every number an instrument

Business reporting and statistics

The people who set the conventions for charts, comparators, notation, tables and plain wording.
12 people

  • Étienne-Jules Marey

    1830–1904 · French physiologist

    1878 La Méthode graphique

    He built instruments that recorded physiological processes directly as curves, and made the case for the graph as the record of an experiment. By collecting graphic methods from other fields, including Ibry’s train schedule, he showed that one pair of axes can hold a whole system of moving parts.

    What to learn from themWhen many items move through the same process, orders through stages or deliveries along a route, give them one pair of axes and draw each as a line, so delays show as changes of slope and conflicts as crossings.

    Rule 7 · Share one axis
  • WBNo free photograph

    Willard Brinton

    1880–1957 · American engineer

    1914 Graphic Methods for Presenting Facts

    He wrote for business people, social workers and legislators rather than statisticians, and showed by example how to choose and draw a chart for a report. He also pushed for common standards, chairing a joint committee of fifteen national societies that published rules for charts in 1915.

    What to learn from themShow quantities as lengths on a common scale, and draw the zero or 100 per cent base line more strongly than the other grid lines, two of the rules his committee published in 1915.

    Rule 2 · Give every number an instrument
  • MRNo free photograph

    Otto Neurath, Marie Reidemeister and Gerd Arntz

    Vienna, from 1925

    1925 Gesellschafts- und Wirtschaftsmuseum

    They built a method for showing social and economic statistics to the general public with standard pictograms, each worth a fixed amount and repeated to show more. They also defined the transformer, the person who turns data and expert knowledge into a chart, a role close to that of an information designer today.

    What to learn from themWhen a count matters, such as 80 pairs of boots not served, show it as repeated units of fixed value that can be counted, and never enlarge one symbol to mean more. Name one person to decide what the chart must say before anyone draws it.

  • Ernest Gowers

    1880–1966 · British civil servant

    1948 Plain Words

    He showed officials, with examples sent in by government departments, how to replace abstract and padded phrases with short, concrete words that members of the public could follow. His revision of Fowler kept a standard guide to English usage current for another generation.

    What to learn from themLabel each figure the way a colleague would say it aloud, lost sales rather than unfilled demand quantity, and test every label by asking someone outside the team what it counts.

    Rule 12 · Use plain words, and say what the numbers are
  • MSNo free photograph

    Mary Eleanor Spear

    1897–1986 · American graphic analyst

    1952 Charting Statistics

    Over some forty years as a graphic analyst for federal agencies, she wrote practical manuals on choosing and drawing charts for official reports, including advice on charts for television. She described charting as work for three roles, an analyst, a draftsman and a communicator, and her range bar anticipated John Tukey’s box plot by almost two decades.

    What to learn from themWhere a row must show the spread of observed values, use a compact range bar: a thin line from lowest to highest, a box for the middle half and a tick for the median, on the same scale as the other rows.

    Rule 10 · Show uncertainty only when it is earned
  • NDNo free photograph

    Nick Desbarats

    Data visualization educator and author, Ottawa

    2022 Why I stopped using bullet graphs

    He turned years of teaching dashboard design into concrete guidelines for choosing and formatting charts in business reports. His critique of the bullet graph argued that a status display should mark only what needs attention, using thresholds that say what action they call for.

    What to learn from themFor each measure on a status board, write down the values at which someone should act, for example crisis, actionably bad and actionably good, and show a mark only when a value crosses one; measures inside the bands need no colour at all.

    Rule 2 · Give every number an instrument

Perception and uncertainty research

Researchers who measured what readers see in a display and decide from it.
9 people

  • Josef Albers

    1888–1976 · German-American artist and teacher

    1933 Black Mountain College

    He taught colour through exercises with coloured paper in which one colour can be made to look like two and two like one, so that colour was studied by observation and experiment. He showed that a colour’s appearance depends on its neighbours, its area and its quantity, and cannot be fixed by its specification alone.

    What to learn from themTest every status colour on each surface it will sit on, such as a white field, a grey panel and dark mode, and adjust it per surface instead of reusing one value and assuming it reads the same everywhere.

    Rule 5 · Shade for hierarchy, print the quantity
  • SPNo free photograph

    Stephen Palmer

    1948–2023 · American psychologist

    1992 Common region

    He added new principles of grouping to the classic Gestalt list, common region and uniform connectedness, and tested them in experiments. Later, with Karen Schloss, he studied why people prefer some colours and how colours and music are linked through emotion.

    What to learn from themDraw a boundary or a shared background only around items that belong together in the reader’s task, because enclosure groups more strongly than nearness; two charts meant to be compared should sit on one surface.

    Rule 5 · Shade for hierarchy, print the quantity
  • CWNo free photograph

    Colin Ware

    Canadian researcher, University of New Hampshire

    1988 Color sequences for univariate maps

    He proposed studying visualization as applied perception, and turned findings on colour, texture, motion and pattern into design guidance with stated reasons. His experiments on colour scales showed that a colour’s surround can be a major source of error when values are read from it.

    What to learn from themLink the views on a screen by brushing: when a reader selects an item in one chart, highlight the same item in every other chart without filtering the rest away.

    Rule 7 · Share one axis
  • MGNo free photograph

    Michael Gleicher

    Computer scientist, University of Wisconsin–Madison

    2011 Visual comparison for information visualization

    He gave designers a small vocabulary for comparison, so a layout can be chosen by deciding whether items sit side by side, are overlaid, or have their difference computed and drawn. With Correll he tested how common encodings of mean and error mislead, and proposed alternatives.

    What to learn from themWhen two plans must be compared, choose deliberately between side by side, overlaid on one axis, or the difference drawn directly; if the decision turns on the gap, plot the gap itself. If an interval is needed, draw it around a point or as a graded band, since a whisker on a filled bar makes values inside the bar look likelier.

    Rule 7 · Share one axisRule 10 · Show uncertainty only when it is earned
  • MSNo free photograph

    Maureen Stone

    American computer scientist, specialist in colour

    2003 A Field Guide to Digital Color

    She joined colour science to visualization practice, showing how size, surround and display change what a colour looks like and how to design palettes that stay distinct. Her work at Tableau carried that research into a product’s default colours.

    What to learn from themCheck every encoding colour at the smallest mark it will fill: a tint that separates two large areas can vanish on a 2 px line or a 6 px dot, so give small marks stronger lightness steps or a second cue.

    Rule 5 · Shade for hierarchy, print the quantityRule 6 · Give each fill one job

  • Tamara Munzner

    Computer scientist, University of British Columbia

    1997 H3

    She gave visualization a vocabulary for analysing a design by what data it shows, why the user needs it and how it is encoded, so choices can be justified and compared. Her nested model separates the levels at which a design can fail, from misunderstanding the problem to choosing a slow algorithm.

    What to learn from themState the user’s task in abstract terms, such as compare, locate or summarise, before choosing a chart; when the task is to compare, put the items side by side on one axis so the comparison is made by eye instead of from memory.

    Rule 7 · Share one axis
  • MKNo free photographJHNo free photograph

    Matthew Kay and Jessica Hullman

    Researchers in uncertainty visualization

    2015 Hypothetical outcome plots

    They built uncertainty displays that use frequency framing, as countable dots or a sequence of possible outcomes, so that readers without statistical training can judge probabilities. They measured the decisions people made with each display, and Kay put these forms into tools that analysts use every day.

    What to learn from themWhen a forecast distribution exists, draw it as about twenty quantile dots so a reader can count the risk: four dots past the stock line means roughly a one-in-five chance of running out.

People who put controls beside their effects, and who studied how plans are really made.

  • Jay Forrester

    1918–2016 · American engineer and systems scientist

    1951 Whirlwind

    He gave planners a notation in which every quantity is either a stock that accumulates or a flow that changes it, with the delays between them made explicit. His supply-chain models showed how orders amplify as they move upstream, the effect first called the Forrester effect and now the bullwhip.

    What to learn from themBefore designing a planning view, write its stock equation (closing stock = opening stock + receipts − sales) and give each term its own mark on one scale, so the reader can see which flow moved the stock.
