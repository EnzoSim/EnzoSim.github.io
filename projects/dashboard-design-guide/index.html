A design guide for planning dashboards

A planning dashboard has to show a chain of arithmetic: what demand asks for, what stock can serve, what is on order, how much budget is left to buy, and what margin results. The craft of drawing such chains is older than the screen. This guide gathers what grid design, relief cartography, control-room engineering, business notation and forecasting research each contribute, explains the sources, and gives a small model to play with for every rule.

One example runs through the guide. A retailer plans four weeks of sales for five product lines. Demand for boots outruns stock in the week of 12 October, because an order of 240 pairs arrives a week too late. Receiving 80 of those pairs a week early would serve the demand. The plan above tells that story in the order a planner works: four instruments, then sales and stock week by week, then the buying budget, then the decisions. Each decision has a switch, and every figure recomputes when one is turned on.

  • About the data
  • Every figure is sample data and is computed in the page. There is no fitted forecast behind it, so the example shows no forecast interval anywhere.

The eleven rules below explain where each decision in that plan comes from. They draw on six bodies of work, each of which has spent decades on one part of the problem.

1

Start from a grid that divides

Karl Gerstner designed the layout grid of the German business magazine Capital in 1962 and published it in Designing Programmes two years later. The page is 58 units wide and the gutters are 2 units. Those two numbers let the same page carry one, two, three, four, five or six columns, each a whole number of units wide. The reason is their sum. Width plus one gutter is 60, and 60 divides by every count from one to six.

  • Karl Gerstner, Designing Programmes, 1964. The Capital grid dates from 1962.

The rule carries straight into pixels. At full width each sheet of the example is 1408 px wide inside, with 8 px gutters. The sum is 1416, which divides by two, three, four, six, eight and twelve. The four instrument cards are therefore 346 px each, and the three-to-two rows split into 840 and 560 px. The week columns have no gutters between them, so the same 1408 px gives eight weeks of 176 px.

A grid that divides many ways matters because the regions of a dashboard should not be equal. Stephen Few lists the rigid symmetrical grid among the common mistakes of dashboard design. When every panel has the same size, the layout claims that every panel has the same importance. A programme of compatible widths allows a wide region for the main evidence and narrow ones for the rest, and no edge falls off the grid.

  • Stephen Few, With Dashboards, Formatting and Layout Definitely Matter, 2008.
2

Give every number an instrument

A number on a card answers one question: how much. A reader of a plan needs four more answers in the same glance: of what, on what scale, compared with what, and by how much it differs. Stephen Few’s bullet graph, specified in 2006, packs those answers into one small mark, with a bar for the measure, a tick for the comparator and a scale behind both.

  • Stephen Few, Bullet Graph Design Specification, 2006, revised 2013.

Control rooms reached a similar form for a different reason. Guidance for process-plant operators asks for grey screens, analog indicators with a marked normal range, and saturated colour reserved for abnormal states, so that a deviation is the only thing that stands out. The instrument in the example borrows that restraint. It shows a large value with its unit, a rail drawn to scale, one ink tick where a comparator exists, and the gap in figures beside the name of what it is measured against.

  • Hollifield, Oliver, Nimmo and Habibi, The High Performance HMI Handbook, 2008. The ISA-101 standard for process displays follows the same discipline.

The bullet graph has critics worth hearing. Nick Desbarats lists eleven objections, among them that problems do not stand out and that words such as good, satisfactory and poor mean different things to different people. The example therefore leaves the qualitative bands out. Where no comparator exists, the rail shows a part of a whole instead: sales made against sales lost, stock held against what is left to buy. Amazon’s weekly business review shows the value of holding one form across hundreds of measures. Every chart plots the trailing six weeks beside the trailing twelve months, so the form is learned once.

  • Nick Desbarats, Why I stopped using bullet graphs, 2022.
  • Colin Bryar and Bill Carr, Working Backwards, 2021, on the weekly business review.
3

Compare against routine variation

A weekly figure always differs from the week before, and most of those differences mean nothing. Donald Wheeler’s Understanding Variation gives a test that needs no model. Plot the individual values, take the average distance between neighbours, and draw limits at 2.66 of those average ranges on either side of the mean. A point inside the limits is routine. A point outside is a signal that deserves an explanation.

  • Donald Wheeler, Understanding Variation, 1993. The chart of individual values and moving ranges descends from Walter Shewhart’s control chart.

This is the comparator an instrument should graduate to once a measure has some history. Until then a named reference, such as the last four weeks, is the more candid choice, and the example uses exactly that.

4

Draw the identity

In 1786 William Playfair drew England’s imports and exports as two lines and tinted the area between them. The tint is the balance of trade. Nobody has to subtract, because the quantity that matters is a shape on the page.

  • William Playfair, The Commercial and Political Atlas, 1786.

Engineers who design displays for power plants turned the same idea into a method. Ecological interface design, set out by Kim Vicente and Jens Rasmussen in 1992, asks a display to make the constraints of the system directly visible. Kevin Bennett and John Flach describe the result as a configural display. Variables are drawn so that their lawful relationship forms a figure, and a fault deforms the figure.

  • Vicente and Rasmussen, Ecological interface design: theoretical foundations, 1992.
  • Bennett and Flach, Display and Interface Design, 2011.

Planning is full of such laws, and open-to-buy is one of them. What a plan needs is the cost of what it expects to sell plus the stock it wants to keep at the end. What it has is the stock on hand plus the stock on order. The difference is what is left to buy. Two bars on one scale state the law, and the dashed space between their ends is the answer. A waterfall chart shows the same numbers and leaves the reader to walk the steps.

5

Shade for hierarchy, print the quantity

Eduard Imhof spent a career drawing Swiss mountain maps and wrote down his rules for colour in Cartographic Relief Presentation. The first one, which Edward Tufte later brought to information design, says that strong colours are unbearable over large adjoining areas and effective in small ones set against a muted ground. A dashboard that keeps its large areas quiet can let one saturated hue do real work.

  • Eduard Imhof, Cartographic Relief Presentation, 1965, quoted in Tufte’s Envisioning Information, 1990.

Two findings from perception explain why quiet surfaces can still organise a screen. Stephen Palmer showed that elements inside a common region are seen as a group, and that enclosure overrides nearness. Every panel boundary is therefore a claim about what belongs together. Josef Albers showed that a colour is read against its surround, so the same grey looks lighter on a dark parent and darker on a light one. A tone can mark a level in a hierarchy. It cannot be trusted to carry a value from one surface to another.

  • Stephen Palmer, Common region: a new principle of perceptual grouping, 1992.
  • Josef Albers, Interaction of Color, 1963.

Tone is also a poor carrier of amount. William Cleveland and Robert McGill ranked the ways a reader decodes a quantity from a chart. Position on a common scale came first and shading came last. The example uses four opaque surface tones, each with one job, and prints the number wherever the amount matters. Translucent panels would give a similar softness, and Beverly Harrison and colleagues studied the trade-off they bring: what shows through helps awareness of the background and competes with reading the foreground.

  • Cleveland and McGill, Graphical perception, 1984, replicated by Heer and Bostock in 2010.
  • Harrison, Ishii, Vicente and Buxton, Transparent layered user interfaces, 1995.
6

Give each fill one job

Most dashboards give colour too many jobs. The accent marks the headline figure, the forecast line, the selected tab and the button. Business reporting has a better convention. The International Business Communication Standards, developed by Rolf Hichert and colleagues and set out with Jürgen Faisst in Solid, Outlined, Hatched, separate scenarios by fill. Actual figures are solid, plans are outlined and forecasts are hatched. Hue is left free.

  • Hichert and Faisst, Solid, Outlined, Hatched, 2019, and the IBCS standard.

The example keeps the principle and changes the means. It makes no claim of compliance, and it uses no hatching, which turns to texture on marks as small as a twelve-pixel rail. Slate is for what has happened or physically exists: sales made, stock on hand. Cobalt is for what lies ahead, and its strength says how firm the quantity is. Solid cobalt is what the plan expects to sell. A lighter tint is what is planned or on its way: stock to keep, orders arriving. An amount that exists only as a gap, such as what is left to buy, is a dashed outline. Lost sales are ink, so a shortfall needs no red. Selection is a raised white pill, so the accent never means clickable.

Jacques Bertin listed value, the lightness of a mark, among his visual variables in 1967, and classed it as ordered: a darker mark reads as more. Strength of fill can therefore carry a ranking that hue cannot. It also survives a grey printer, a washed-out projector and a reader who cannot tell two hues apart.

  • Jacques Bertin, Semiology of Graphics, 1967. The link summarises his visual variables.
7

Share one axis

In 1885 Étienne-Jules Marey published a railway timetable as a single graph, using a method devised by the engineer Ibry. Stations run down the side by distance, time runs along the bottom, and every train is a line whose slope is its speed. A whole day of departures becomes one picture because every train shares the same two axes.

  • Étienne-Jules Marey, La Méthode Graphique, 1885. The link is a modern redrawing of the form.

Jacques Bertin made the same point for tables. His reorderable matrix draws each cell as a mark and then moves rows and columns until the structure shows. Richard Becker, William Cleveland and Ming-Jen Shyu formalised panels that share scales as the trellis display, and Waqas Javed and colleagues later found that separate small charts are generally more efficient than one shared chart for comparisons that span many series. Weather services use the form every day. The ensemble meteogram stacks cloud, wind, rain and temperature on one time axis.

  • Bertin’s matrix, revisited by Perin, Dragicevic and Fekete in Bertifier, 2014.
  • Becker, Cleveland and Shyu, The visual design and control of Trellis display, 1996.
  • Javed, McDonnel and Elmqvist, Graphical perception of multiple time series, 2010.
  • ECMWF, ensemble meteograms.

Retail planners have their own version, the weekly sales, stock and intake grid, with weeks across and measures down. The example keeps its week columns at the same horizontal position in the sales bars and in every stock row, so the order that arrives too late sits directly under the week it fails. David Woods gave the underlying quality a name, visual momentum: how easily a reader carries what was just seen into the next view.

  • The weekly sales, stock and intake grid, a standard tool of merchandise planning.
  • David Woods, Visual momentum, 1984.
8

Set tables to be read

Planners live in tables, and research has begun to notice. Lyn Bartram, Michael Correll and Melanie Tory found that people who work with data want to see and touch the table at every stage of analysis, and argued that the table is a visualization in its own right. The rules for setting one are old and consistent.

  • Bartram, Correll and Tory, Untidy data: the unreasonable effectiveness of tables, 2022.

Jan Tschichold advised doing without rules where possible, and using vertical ones only when columns are too tight to read without them. Robert Bringhurst asks that a table be edited like text and its furniture kept to a minimum. Andrew Ehrenberg’s Rudiments of Numeracy adds the rules for the figures themselves: round to the two digits that actually vary, order the rows by size, and put the numbers to be compared in a column. Jonathan Schwabish’s checklist says the same in current terms. Align numbers to the right, state units once, lighten the lines.

  • Tschichold and Bringhurst on tables, summarised by Richard Rutter, 2017.
  • Andrew Ehrenberg, Rudiments of numeracy, 1977.
  • Jonathan Schwabish, Ten guidelines for better tables, 2020.

A table can also carry marks without ceasing to be a table. The Table Lens of Ramana Rao and Stuart Card drew a small bar inside each cell, and LineUp later built whole rankings from such bars. Plain typography goes a long way too. The Dutch telephone directory designed by Wim Crouwel and Jolijn van de Wouw in 1977 set every line in condensed Univers, in lower case, with the number placed before the name so that no leader dots were needed.

  • Rao and Card, The Table Lens, 1994.
  • Gratzl and colleagues, LineUp, 2013.
  • Crouwel and van de Wouw, Dutch telephone directory, 1977.
9

Choose type for figures

Dense figures have had typefaces of their own for decades. Matthew Carter drew Bell Centennial in the 1970s for American telephone directories printed fast on poor paper. Tobias Frere-Jones drew Retina in 1999 for the stock tables of the Wall Street Journal. Both are instruments for small numbers, and both are worth a look before choosing a face for a grid.

  • Matthew Carter, Bell Centennial, 1975 to 1978.
  • Tobias Frere-Jones, Retina, 1999, in the collection of the Museum of Modern Art.

On screen the requirements are few and testable. Figures must be tabular, so every digit has the same width and columns align. They should also be duplexed, meaning a digit keeps its width when the weight changes. A selected row can then turn bold without its columns moving. A family drawn as a system helps as well. Adrian Frutiger organised Univers in 1957 as a matrix of weights and widths, and a variable font gives the same range from one file.

  • Adrian Frutiger, Univers, 1957. Twenty-one variants named by weight and width.
  • Instrument Sans, the face used for every figure on this page.

Seventeen open typefaces were measured for this guide in a browser. Instrument Sans, IBM Plex Sans, Hanken Grotesk and Red Hat Text keep their digit width between regular and bold. Geist, Archivo and Roboto Flex do not. Punctuation is worth measuring too. In Instrument Sans the digits, the minus and the percent sign have one width in both weights, while the comma and the decimal point are about a thirtieth of an em wider in bold, which is half a pixel at 15 px.

10

Show uncertainty only when it is earned

A forecast drawn as a single line claims more than anyone knows. The Bank of England made the point in February 1996, when it began publishing its inflation forecast as a fan of nested bands, a form chosen to show the spread of likely outcomes without suggesting a spurious precision. Weather services draw the spread of an ensemble forecast as box-and-whisker steps along the time axis.

  • Britton, Fisher and Whitley, The Inflation Report projections: understanding the fan chart, 1998.

Research since then favours forms a reader can count. Matthew Kay and colleagues introduced the quantile dotplot, which turns a distribution into twenty or so dots of equal probability. In a later experiment led by Michael Fernandes, such displays improved decisions about when to leave for a bus. Michael Correll and Michael Gleicher found that a bar with a whisker misleads, because values inside the bar look likelier than values just above it. Susan Joslyn and Jared LeClerc found that showing uncertainty improved decisions and reduced the damage done by a forecast that turned out wrong.

  • Kay, Kola, Hullman and Munson, When (ish) is My Bus?, 2016.
  • Fernandes and colleagues, Uncertainty displays using quantile dotplots or CDFs improve transit decision-making, 2018.
  • Correll and Gleicher, Error bars considered harmful, 2014.
  • Joslyn and LeClerc, Uncertainty forecasts improve weather-related decisions, 2012.

None of this licenses a band on a chart that has no model behind it. The example is built on sample data with no fitted forecast, so it shows no interval anywhere. Its solid cobalt bars are what the plan’s arithmetic expects, and a dotplot can take their place on the day a forecast with measured error exists.

11

Put each control on its consequence

Ben Shneiderman’s work on dynamic queries set the standard for interactive data in the 1990s. Controls stay visible, every change is small and reversible, and the display answers at once. Bret Victor’s Explorable Explanations carried the idea into prose, with numbers in a sentence that the reader can drag while the conclusion rewrites itself.

  • Ben Shneiderman, Dynamic queries for visual information seeking, 1994.
  • Bret Victor, Explorable Explanations, 2011.

The New York Times calculator Is It Better to Rent or Buy?, built in 2014 by Mike Bostock, Shan Carter and Archie Tse, adds the decisive detail. Each slider sits on a small chart of the outcome across the whole range of that input, so the reader sees what a change would do before making it. The markdown plot below goes one step further and needs no demand model. Its curve is the number of tents that must sell at each discount to match the profit at full price. The test is a point on that plot, and it is dragged where it stands, so the control and its consequence are one object.

  • Bostock, Carter and Tse, Is It Better to Rent or Buy?, The New York Times, 2014.

Changing a forecast by hand deserves the same care as any other control. Robert Fildes and colleagues studied judgmental adjustments in supply-chain companies. Small adjustments tended to reduce accuracy, large reasoned ones helped, and adjustments leaned optimistic. An override should ask for a reason and keep a record, so that its value can be reviewed later.

  • Fildes, Goodwin, Lawrence and Nikolopoulos, Effective forecasting and judgmental adjustments, 2009.
  • Michael Gilliland, Forecast value added: a reality check, 2013.

The system in brief

The example is built from a small set of tokens and rules. They are listed here so the design can be rebuilt or adapted, and the figure below lets three of them move.

GridSheets 1408 px wide inside, with 8 px gutters and an 8 px inset. Four instrument cards, eight week columns of 176 px, and rows split three to two.
SurfacesGround, sheet, field and well. Higher is lighter. An inner radius is the outer radius minus the inset: 20 for a sheet, 12 for a field.
TypeOne family. Values 48 px at weight 600, text and figures 15 px, labels 14 px, ticks 13 px. Tabular, duplexed figures.
FiguresA bold value over a grey label at each mark. One decimal for money in thousands. A real minus sign. Plain words: lost sales, weeks of stock, left to buy.
ColourSlate for what is sold or in stock. One accent for what is expected, its tint for what is planned or arriving, a dashed outline for what is left to buy. Ink for lost sales. No red.
InteractionControls sit on the chart they change. Pointing highlights and never filters. Every value is printed, so nothing depends on hover.
