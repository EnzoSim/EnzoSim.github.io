Design literature and precedents for Altitude

A first research guide, not published here, gathered about twenty authorities and translated each into advice for a retail demand-planning dashboard. This edition checks that work, adds roughly ninety sources from eight neighbouring fields, and ends with one proposed system drawn with the figures of the dashboard’s second version (V2). Its argument is that the reference look is a way of drawing accounting identities with instrument precision on quiet tonal surfaces, and that for retail planning it should move from separate cards to one shared weekly sheet.

Part 1

What holds in the first guide, and what does not

The claims the first guide leans on most were re-checked. Most hold. Three need a correction. The larger weakness is one of shape: the guide is a catalogue of authorities with a translation attached to each, so it never has to choose between them, and it never leaves graphic design and visualization to ask what retail planners and forecasters already know.

    Six gaps that matter more than any single claim

    No forecasting or uncertainty literatureThe dashboard is about forecasts, yet the guide cites nothing on how forecasts and their error should be drawn.
    No retail planning practiceThe weekly sales, stock and intake grid, intermittent demand, and the research on judgmental overrides are absent.
    No table designPlanners work in grids. The guide treats tables as a fallback behind charts.
    No research on dashboards themselvesTwo systematic studies of dashboard types and patterns exist and are not used.
    No perceptual basis for the surfacesThe soft panels are described but never explained: why enclosure groups, and what translucency costs.
    No dissentThe bullet graph is the guide's central precedent, and its best-known published critique is missing.

    Gerstner's arithmetic, checked and carried into pixels

    The guide reports the Capital grid correctly: 58 units wide with 2-unit gutters, dividing into one to six columns. The reason it works is worth stating, because it gives a reusable rule. Add one gutter to the width and the result is 60, which 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6 all divide. Any field whose width plus one gutter is a multiple of 60 has the same property.

    Part 2

    Reading the reference design language

    The Réseau Environnement reference is the author’s own water-budget dashboard, so the first guide was partly describing an existing taste. A more useful question is which traditions that taste belongs to. Each one comes with rules to borrow and limits to know about. Four mechanisms can be read in the reference, and each has a longer history than the first guide suggests.

    What this reading adds

    The second mechanism is the one the first guide undervalued. It called the shaded bridge an annotation. It is stronger than that. In Coûts et financement the two bars have the same length because costs equal revenues, and the bracket isolates the fixed costs that volume revenue has to carry. Engineers who design control-room displays have a name for this: a configural display, where a law of the system is drawn as a shape and a violation shows up as a broken shape. Open-to-buy is exactly such a law, and Part 5 draws it that way.

    The first mechanism has a closer relative than the bullet graph. Process-industry guidance since 2008 asks for grey screens, analog indicators that show a normal range, and saturated colour held back for abnormal states. That is the same palette discipline, arrived at for safety reasons.

    Where the interpretation can be challenged

    The density does not transfer. By a manual count the reference crop carries about 32 figures in 886 × 374 px, one figure per 100 × 100 px. V2 carries about 135, tick labels included, on 1440 × 1568 px, which is sparser still. A weekly planning grid with 72 × 28 px cells is five to eight times denser than either. The reference feels rich because each figure is carefully related to another, not because there are many of them. Planning needs both.

    Cards are the wrong unit for a chain. Palmer's work on common region explains why the reference panels group so firmly: a boundary tells the eye that what is inside belongs together and what is outside does not. V2 puts demand, cover, stock, open-to-buy and margin in seven separate boxes, so the layout says they are seven topics. They are one chain. Few made the same point about dashboards forced into equal quadrants, and Woods called the cost of jumping between unrelated displays a loss of visual momentum.

    Blue has four jobs. In V2 it marks the headline value, the modelled demand line, the receipts and the active tab. Business reporting solved this with fill instead of hue: solid for actual, outlined for plan, hatched for forecast. That frees the one accent to mean a single thing.

    Translucent without blur is a tint. The reference surfaces are white at 84% over a grey ground. Harrison and colleagues measured what real translucency costs in legibility. The reference has none of the benefit, since nothing shows through, so specify opaque tones and keep the look.

    Softness has a nesting limit. A 24 px radius with a 24 px inset works for one level. At three levels the inner corners no longer follow the outer ones and the padding consumes the field. Part 5 uses concentric radii: each inner radius is the outer radius minus the inset.

    A more compelling direction

    Retail planning already has a native graphic, the weekly sales, stock and intake grid. Forecasting has another, the meteogram, which stacks several parameters on one time axis. Swiss relief cartography supplies the shading discipline. Put together they suggest a relief sheet: a few wide rows, each an instrument on the left and its evidence on the right, with weeks aligned from row to row and the rows ordered as the planning chain runs. Cards remain for things that really are separate.

    Part 3

    Wider research: what the first guide missed

    The first guide stayed inside graphic design, visualization and a few product examples. The sources below come from eight neighbouring fields. Each entry says what it gives this project. The references the first guide already holds (Tufte, Munzner, Heer and Shneiderman, Victor, the New York Times calculator, Carbon, SAP, Toolio, Palantir, JMP, ParaView, MathWorks) stay valid and are not repeated.

    Forecasts and their uncertainty

    V2 claims no fitted model, so the honest display today has no interval at all. The system still needs a reserved place for one. When a forecast with measured error exists, the evidence favours discrete, countable outcomes over error bars: quantile dotplots improved decisions in transit experiments, and bars with whiskers bias readers toward values inside the bar. Meteorology supplies the layout, with several parameters on one time axis and each with its own spread.

      Business notation and accounting graphics

      Business reporting has its own notation standard. IBCS separates actual, plan and forecast by fill and leaves colour free, which answers the first guide's worry about blue. Amazon's weekly review format and Wheeler's process charts add a discipline V2 lacks, which is telling a real change from routine weekly variation. The bullet graph also has a serious critic.

        Retail planning and forecasting practice

        The research literature here speaks directly to interface choices. Fildes and colleagues studied thousands of forecast overrides in supply-chain companies: small adjustments tended to reduce accuracy, large reasoned ones helped, and adjustments leaned optimistic. An override control should therefore ask for a reason and record what the change added. Sport Obermeyer, a skiwear maker, is the classic case of buying seasonal outdoor apparel before demand is known.

          Tables, matrices and multiples

          Planners read tables, and Bartram, Correll and Tory argue that visualization research has neglected them. The craft advice is consistent across a century: few rules and only horizontal ones, numbers aligned and rounded to what matters, rows ordered by size. Bertin's reorderable matrix and its descendants show how a table becomes a graphic without losing its figures.

            Perception, shading and colour

            Three findings should govern the surfaces. Enclosure groups more strongly than proximity. A tint is read relative to its surround, so the same grey means different things on different parents. Shade is a weak carrier of quantity, so use it for hierarchy and print the number when the amount matters.

              Displays for operators

              This is the least expected lineage, and the one closest to the reference instruments. Control-room designers reached grey surfaces, reference ranges and reserved colour through incident analysis, and they developed a theory of drawing a system's constraints as geometry.

                Dashboards, views and comparison as research subjects

                Several of the first guide's open questions have published answers: when to split information into views, how comparison designs are built, how parameter exploration is structured, and how much text readers want on a chart. The last one matters for the reference style. Readers prefer annotated charts, which supports text placed at the mark and argues against stripping it out.

                  Type for figures

                  Typefaces drawn for dense numeric listings exist and are instructive. Seventeen open faces were also tested in a browser for one property the planning grid needs: figures that keep their width when the weight changes, so a selected row can go bold without its columns shifting. The result is in Part 5.

                    Remarkable works and interfaces

                    These are worth opening and studying as whole artefacts. Two come from the outdoor world Altitude sells into, which makes them useful as vernacular as well as precedent.

                      Part 4

                      Three directions drawn on the same slice

                      All three use the same dataset and answer the same question: why does Salomon miss demand in week 42, and what does the early purchase order change? V2 is the baseline.

                      A · Instrument cards

                      The reference grammar carried across faithfully: large rounded cards, cobalt numerals, a rail with a reference tick, a bridge and aligned scenario rows.

                      B · Ledger

                      The planner's own form: a weekly grid with measures as rows, set by the table rules in Part 3 and marked with solid and outlined column keys.

                      C · Relief sheet recommended

                      Four rows follow the planning chain: demand, service, buying capacity, margin. Each row is an instrument and its evidence on one surface. Weeks 40 to 43 sit at the same horizontal position in the first two rows. Switch the plan and every figure is recomputed from the dataset. Point at a week to trace it across rows.

                      Why it works: nesting follows the arithmeticEach sheet holds one link of the chain. The white field inside it holds the evidence. A reader who understands the surfaces understands the model.
                      Why it works: one axis, read downDemand, receipts and closing stock for a week share a column, so the cause of the shortfall sits directly above its effect.
                      Why it works: the gap is drawn, not colouredUnfilled demand is the empty part of an outlined bar. Open-to-buy is the dashed space between what is needed and what is covered. No red is required.
                      Where it is weakerIt needs about 1100 px of width to keep eight week columns readable. Below that the rows stack and the week strip scrolls. It also asks the reader to learn four fills.
                      Part 5

                      The proposed system

                      Everything in this part is a proposal. It is written so it can be built: the measurements are whole numbers, the tokens are listed, and the rules say what to do when content changes.

                      Grid: a programme of 60

                      The field is 1424 px with 16 px gutters. Field plus one gutter is 1440, which is 60 modules of 24 px. One to six columns all land on whole multiples of 8. A 264 px instrument column and a 1136 px evidence field sit inside each sheet with 8 px insets, and the evidence field divides into eight gutterless week columns of 142 px. Vertical rhythm is 4 px, with rows of 28, 36 and 44 px.

                      Typography: one family, two widths, duplexed figures

                      Instrument Sans has a width axis from 75 to 100. Text and figures use the normal width. Column heads and micro labels use 85, which buys space without a second family. Frutiger organised Univers the same way in 1957, as a matrix of weight by width.

                      The highlighted row is bold. Its figures stay on the same right edges because regular and bold digits have one width.

                      Measured on 1 October 2026 in headless Chrome, Google Fonts builds, tabular figures, width of “0” in em at 400 and 700.
                      Keep width: Instrument Sans 0.600 / 0.600 · IBM Plex Sans 0.600 / 0.600 · Hanken Grotesk 0.560 / 0.560 · Red Hat Text 0.600 / 0.600.
                      Shift when bold: Geist 0.600 / 0.636 · Archivo 0.568 / 0.598 · Roboto Flex 0.444 / 0.546.
                      In the faces that keep width the minus sign also matches the digit width, so signed columns align.
                      Value and unit never separate“294.4 C$k” is one unbroken string. The unit is 13 px beside a 34 px value, on the same baseline.
                      One decimal for money in thousandsEhrenberg's rule: round to the digits that vary. Units are whole numbers with a thousands comma.
                      A real minus signU+2212, never a hyphen. Deficits are ink with a minus, never a colour.
                      Context lives in the title lineScope, unit and period sit beside the title in grey. No floating captions inside a field.

                      Surfaces: five opaque tones and concentric corners

                      Higher is lighter. Each tone has one job. The radius of a nested surface is its parent's radius minus the inset between them, so corners stay parallel at any depth.

                      Notation: fill carries status, hue carries time

                      Adapted from IBCS and not a claim of compliance. Ink is what has happened or physically exists. Cobalt is everything forward-looking. Within cobalt, the fill says how firm the quantity is. Selection is an ink outline and a raised white pill, so cobalt never means “clickable”.

                      The reserved slot. Hatched means projected by the plan's arithmetic. When a fitted forecast with measured error exists, its spread is drawn as a quantile dotplot or nested bands inside the same outlined bar. Until then no band, whisker or shaded cone appears anywhere, because V2 has nothing to justify one.

                      Components

                      InstrumentLabel and context, value and unit, a rail on a true scale, an ink tick for the named comparator, the signed gap. If no comparator exists the rail is omitted, as with GMROI here.
                      BridgeTwo bars on one scale whose lengths are tied by an identity. The open amount is the dashed space between their ends. Use it only where an identity really holds.
                      Week stripEight 142 px columns. Actual weeks are solid ink. Plan weeks are an outline with a hatched served portion. A dashed rule marks the snapshot.
                      Pair matrixRows are brand and category pairs, columns are week ends. Each cell prints closing units. Tint shows how much of the four-week horizon the stock would not cover. Receipts are cobalt. A cell with unfilled demand is solid ink.
                      Decision rowWhat is asked, its cash timing at cost, its gross-profit effect, and what remains unconfirmed. A request is never drawn as approved.
                      Assumption with responseA control placed on its own consequence. The tent example plots the units needed to hold gross profit at each markdown depth, which is arithmetic and needs no demand model.

                      Interaction

                      Plan switch on the chart it changesA segmented control with self-standing names. The fixed reference stays visible as the instrument tick.
                      Week and pair tracingPointing at a week highlights that column in every time-based row. It highlights and never filters, so context stays.
                      Overrides leave a traceAny manual change to demand asks for a reason and is logged for a later value-added review, after Fildes and Gilliland.
                      Keyboard and no-hover parityEvery value is printed. Hover adds emphasis only. Controls are native buttons and range inputs.

                      What would change this recommendation
                      
    
                      If the main audience is a hiring panel that will look for thirty seconds, lead with Direction A as a cover and link to C as the working view. If the case grows from five pairs to hundreds of items, the matrix needs sorting, grouping and a horizon-chart mode, and Direction B carries more of the load. If a fitted forecast arrives, the reserved slot opens and the instrument comparators change from “previous four weeks” to “forecast”. None of this has been tested with planners, and it should be before any of it is called better than V2.
